One family makes the ticket's own account of itself true; the other makes the check that reads it consistent, because today the same situation refuses some tickets and quietly passes others."
description: "A ticket inside a body of related work completes every step, and the run then refuses to mark it finished. The message says nothing is outstanding and refuses anyway, which is why three consecutive runs were spent on it before the pattern was visible. Two halves are each individually right and were never introduced to each other: the run deliberately skips the pull-request step for a ticket inside an epic, because one request covers the whole branch, and the generator writes that step into every ticket it produces because it does not know where the ticket will end up. The first family covers the generator, and its central criterion is written as agreement rather than as a particular value — the steps the record names must equal the steps the run will take for that ticket's location — with a scenario that changes which step is skipped and demands the record follow without anyone editing code, so hard-coding one step's name in one branch fails. It also fixes the three cheapest wrong answers, each of which clears the halt while leaving the defect: recording the skipped step as signed off, which is a false statement in the very evidence the check reads; leaving it out of the record entirely, which works because the check walks entries; and skipping it everywhere, which breaks every standalone ticket and passes any test that only looks at epic ones. Authoring it surfaced something that would have made the obvious fix useless: every epic ticket is written into the inbox first and moved into its epic folder afterwards, so a generator that asks where it is being written gets the answer not-an-epic for every one of them, while a test that hands it an epic-shaped destination passes. The criteria therefore require the caller to state the final destination, and forbid inferring it from the write location. The second family covers the check itself, and exists because the check has two doors. One runs a script that compares what the ticket says against what is signed off and refuses; the other edits the ticket directly and never compares. The same condition therefore refused one ticket and marked two others finished in a single run, and the lenient outcome is the worse of the two because it records work as complete on the strength of nothing. The criteria close the second door rather than discouraging it, keep the refusal intact, and say which answer both doors must give. What they explicitly reject is the tempting repair of having the check simply believe the run's list of steps to ignore: a check that accepts the account of the party it is checking has stopped checking, and the refusal is worth something only while the record is written by someone else. Measuring the population changed the shape of the work. The register recorded twenty-five affected tickets in one body of work and a hand correction already applied; there are three hundred and thirty-three across twenty-four, and the correction lives on a branch that was never merged. So making the check consistent does not rescue anything — it makes the block reliable where today roughly two in three slip past into a false finish. That is the right behaviour and a real cost, and it means a mechanical repair of the existing population has to run before the consistency lands, or the next run stops on the first of three hundred and sixteen."
